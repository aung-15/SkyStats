About the Project

Meet the Team

SkyStats is a final project for DH 101 (Digital Humanities), Summer 2026, built by a six-person team exploring how COVID-19 reshaped commercial air travel in the United States.

Our Goal

Numbers Aren't the Whole Story

Our team's goal is to bring a humanistic perspective to the T-100 dataset, allowing aviation to be viewed as more than a spreadsheet. Airlines, passenger counts, load factors, and route profitability are all variables drawn from the database, but our motivation lies in the behavioral and social reasons behind those numbers. What did it mean that leisure travel rebounded almost immediately after lockdowns lifted while business travel struggled for years? What does it say about the people living through the pandemic that some metro areas' airports recovered while others, like Los Angeles, did not?

With this project, we wanted to push past a narrative that treats air traffic purely as numbers. By tracing how "revenge travel" unfolded, we hoped to understand who was flying, why they chose to fly, and how those behavioral choices reshaped the airline route network from before the pandemic to after it.

Methodology

The Three Levels of This Project

Every digital humanities project is built at three interdependent levels — sources, processing, and presentation — and the decisions made at each level shape what questions can be asked and answered. As Johanna Drucker writes, "the source level of the project refers not only to materials gathered by the researcher, but also to the decisions made in selecting and organizing them."1 Here is our accounting of those decisions.

1. Sources

Our primary source is the T-100 Segment dataset from the U.S. Bureau of Transportation Statistics. To study how COVID-19 affected air travel and how recovery proceeded differently across the airline network, we used data from 2019 through 2025. 2019 was chosen as our baseline because it is the last full year without pandemic-related disruption, letting us measure both the initial 2020 collapse and the degree of recovery through 2025.

This dataset suited our project because it let us examine passenger activity from several angles at once: flight distance let us compare short-, medium-, and long-haul travel; origin and destination fields gave us route-level information; and yearly passenger totals let us trace the recovery process over time. Secondary sources from our annotated bibliography supply the historical, political, economic, and social background that raw traffic numbers cannot — they explain why the patterns we found occurred, in relation to public health restrictions, changing behavior, economic uncertainty, and reopening policy. Choosing the T-100 dataset, concentrating on 2019–2025, setting 2019 as the baseline, and organizing records by distance, passengers, and routes were all decisions that shaped which questions we could ask and how we could interpret our subject.

2. Processing

Data was processed and analyzed primarily in R, which let us combine multi-year airline data, produce summaries, compare categories, and generate visualizations in one cohesive analytical pipeline (Tableau and Flourish were used for several of our maps and charts). We first loaded and combined the yearly datasets so we could analyze change across the full 2019–2025 period, then selected the variables relevant to our research questions: year, month, origin, destination, distance, passengers, and related indicators of airline activity.

Because our raw data consists of individual flight segments, we had to organize it in ways that could answer humanities questions, not just report totals. For distance analysis, we split flights into three groups — short-haul (under 500 miles), medium-haul (500–1,499 miles), and long-haul (1,500 miles or more) — because without grouping, there would be too many distinct distances to compare meaningfully. We then calculated a Passenger Recovery Index for each category:

Passenger Recovery Index

Passenger Total in Selected Year ÷ Passenger Total in 2019 × 100. An index of 100 means activity equals 2019 levels; below 100 means travel is still suppressed; above 100 means it has exceeded its pre-pandemic baseline.

Route-level analysis required a different process. We compared each year's active routes against 2019's to build a Route Restoration Index — the share of 2019 routes still active in a given year — and separately a Common Route Traffic Index — passenger activity only on routes that existed in both 2019 and the selected year. Distinguishing these two indexes was essential to our findings: it let us tell whether recovery came from restoring the airline network's geographic reach, or simply from concentrating more passengers onto the routes that survived. Processing was not just data cleaning; as Drucker argues, computational processing forces the researcher to make categories, relations, and analytical constructs.2 Our distance categories, recovery index, route comparisons, and 2019 baseline were all constructed analytical choices, and we documented each of our calculations so readers can judge for themselves whether they were suitable for the questions we asked.

3. Presentation

SkyStats is designed as a web-based mini-site combining written narrative, data visualizations, a map, a timeline, an annotated bibliography, a data critique, and project and author information. This layout lets readers move back and forth between numerical evidence, geographic patterns, historical background, and written interpretation, rather than encountering charts as disconnected graphics. Every visualization on our Narrative page is embedded directly in the surrounding text and answers a specific humanities question; none stand alone without explanation of what a reader should notice, how it relates to our research questions, and why it matters.

The site is written for an educated general audience rather than aviation specialists: technical variable names are translated into plain language, and every chart carries a title, axis labels, a caption, and a source. As Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic argues, a good data story presents only the information necessary to make its point, without excess visuals distracting the audience3 — so we kept each visualization focused on the single question it was built to answer, favoring reference lines, annotations, and consistent category ordering over decoration. This also follows Drucker's claim that digital interfaces and visualizations are interpretative structures, not a neutral container for data.4 The ordering of our pages, the placement of our charts, our choice of chart types, and the transitions in our written narrative all actively construct the meaning of our evidence — they do not simply display it.

1, 2, 4. Drucker, Johanna. "Humanities Approaches to Graphical Display." Digital Humanities Quarterly 5, no. 1 (2011).  3. Knaflic, Cole Nussbaumer. Storytelling with Data: A Data Visualization Guide for Business Professionals. Hoboken: Wiley, 2015.

How We Worked

Process & Collaboration

Our team communicated daily through a shared group chat and met regularly to check in on roles and milestones, storing all project files and drafts in a shared Google Drive. We divided labor across the roles above while staying flexible — several team members contributed to data work, writing, and design as the project evolved.

Decisions were made by majority rule with a strong preference for compromise, and we prioritized starting tasks early so there was always time left to review each other's work before deadlines.
