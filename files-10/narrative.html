Narrative

Tracing the Collapse and the Comeback

A humanities reading of the T-100 dataset: what the collapse and recovery of air travel reveal about who was flying, why, and which places got left behind.

Introduction

The impact of the pandemic on commercial air travel matters to everyone in the aviation industry trying to adjust their business models to a new normal of traffic patterns. Many studies have examined this from a business perspective — how airlines responded to shifting market dynamics — but far fewer studies of comparable depth have been conducted from a humanistic perspective. That gap matters: the behaviors of passengers changed a great deal during and after COVID-19, and ignoring them has a worrying real-world consequence, since the number of disruptive passengers reported to the Federal Aviation Administration rose sharply after the pandemic, contributing to delayed and canceled flights. We wanted to look at the effect of COVID-19 from the perspective of passengers — the "main character" that quantitative research on this topic often shrinks to a number — and to explore the human justifications behind the business decisions airlines made, and the social reasons behind the rise of so-called "revenge travel."

Our team's motivation is to bring a humanistic perspective to the T-100 dataset, treating aviation as more than a spreadsheet of airlines, passenger counts, and load factors. We wanted to use this data to trace how the pandemic reshaped not just where planes flew, but why people got on them, and what that tells us about how flight itself was understood at that moment in history. Our research question follows directly from that goal:

How did COVID-19 travel restrictions affect commercial air passenger volume, flight distances, and route networks across the ten largest U.S. metropolitan areas — and to what extent, and how evenly, had these patterns recovered by 2025?

Literature Review

Existing literature on aviation during COVID-19 largely aims to explain how the industry changed since the outbreak. A recurring finding is that classical statistical demand-forecasting models stopped working once travel restrictions began shifting faster than the models could adapt (Jafari 2022). Scholars generally agree that business travel recovered slowly as remote work became normalized, while leisure travel rebounded almost immediately once lockdowns eased — a rebound dramatic enough that researchers coined the term "revenge travel." Where the literature disagrees is on whether this shift is temporary or permanent, and who benefited most from it. Some scholars argue legacy carriers and major hub airports won passengers back fastest on the strength of larger, wealthier catchment populations. Others, including Higgins-Desbiolles (2022), argue that low-cost carriers benefited more because "the pandemic also sparked greater focus on the value of regional tourism and travel," pulling travelers toward secondary airports and direct regional routes.

We think many questions remain open. Revenge travel brought much-needed revenue relief to the industry, but its long-term sustainability is untested, and it is not clear whether airlines can generate enough revenue from leisure travelers to fully replace lost business clients — a possibility supported by Javadinasr et al. (2022)'s national panel survey, in which 48% of respondents expected to retain the option to work from home after the pandemic and 41% of pre-pandemic business travelers expected to fly less going forward. That is the gap our project sits in: not whether air travel recovered, but what its uneven recovery reveals about how people's priorities and geography changed.

How Many People Flew, and How Full Were the Planes?

Our first humanities question is the most direct one: how much did the pandemic actually reduce flying, and did the industry recover by restoring its old schedule or by rethinking it? Across the ten metro areas we studied, total annual passengers fell 61.8% between 2019 and 2020 — a drop with no precedent in the modern history of U.S. commercial aviation — before climbing back past the 2019 baseline by 2024.

Total National Passenger Volume by Year

Sum of monthly passengers across Atlanta, Chicago, Dallas–Fort Worth, Houston, Los Angeles, Miami, New York, Philadelphia, Phoenix, and Washington DC, 2019–2025. Source: BTS T-100 Segment, aggregated by our team.

But a rebound in total passengers is not the same as a rebound in service. Total departures across our ten metro areas were still 9.1% below 2019 levels in 2023, even as passenger counts had nearly closed the gap — meaning the average flight was carrying significantly more people than it had before the pandemic. Our team's broader analysis of the T-100 dataset's domestic/international split confirms this directly: average domestic passengers per departure fell from 95 in 2019 to 65 during the 2020 collapse, then climbed past its pre-pandemic level to roughly 106 by 2023 before settling near 101 in 2025. International flights followed the same arc, from 150 down to 110 and back up to about 158.

Average Passengers per Departure, Domestic vs. International

Figures from our team's domestic/international T-100 breakdown analysis (2023 international figure not separately reported). When compared against total departures remaining below 2019 levels, this indicates airlines met recovering demand by concentrating it onto fewer, larger, fuller aircraft rather than restoring the full pre-pandemic flight schedule.

This is where a purely operational reading of the data and a humanities reading start to diverge. Fewer flights carrying more people per departure is, in one sense, a more fuel-efficient system — fewer of the takeoff-and-landing cycles that are the most fuel-intensive phase of flight. But it is also a less convenient one for travelers, especially those in smaller cities who may have lost direct service entirely. What reads in an operations report as improved efficiency reads, from a passenger's seat, as fewer choices and more crowded planes.

Did the Shape of the Year Come Back?

A second question is whether the pandemic permanently changed the rhythm of air travel, or only paused it. Comparing monthly passenger totals for 2019, 2020, and 2025 side by side shows something striking: the 2025 line retraces the 2019 seasonal curve almost exactly — the same summer peak, the same early-year dip — even in a year when several individual metro areas still had not returned to their 2019 passenger volumes.

Monthly Passenger Volume: 2019 vs. 2020 vs. 2025

National sum across our ten metro areas. April 2020 is the low point of the entire dataset, at roughly 1.4 million passengers — a 96% collapse from April 2019.

In other words, the pandemic reshaped how much people flew and, as we show next, where, but it did not permanently reshape when in the year Americans choose to travel. That stability is itself a humanities finding: it suggests the cultural rhythm of summer vacation and holiday travel proved more durable than the industry's route network or fleet schedules.

Which Places Came Back, and Which Didn't?

National totals average away a much more uneven story underneath. Plotting all ten metro areas' monthly passenger totals together reveals that the pandemic reordered which cities anchor American air travel, at least temporarily. New York, which normally carries the most passengers of any metro in our dataset, was overtaken for all of the second half of 2020 by Dallas–Fort Worth — a conservative state with comparatively fewer travel restrictions than New York's. By contrast, Miami has popped up as the second-busiest metro in our dataset every January and February since 2022, driven by winter tourism, before dropping back down for the rest of the year. And Los Angeles, still the second-largest metro in our dataset by population, was overtaken by Atlanta for the full 2025 calendar year — the clearest sign in our data that recovery was not simply proportional to a city's pre-pandemic size.

Monthly Passenger Traffic by Metro Area, 2019–2025

All ten metro areas, monthly, with a reference line at March 2020 marking the U.S. national emergency declaration. Notice Dallas–Fort Worth's lead through the second half of 2020 and Miami's recurring winter spikes.

Zooming out from month-to-month swings to the full five-year picture, the unevenness becomes even clearer. Comparing each metro's 2025 passenger total against its own 2019 baseline shows that four of our ten metro areas — Atlanta, Chicago, Los Angeles, and Philadelphia — still had not returned to their pre-pandemic volume as of 2025, even though the national sum had.

Passenger Volume Change, 2025 vs. 2019, by Metro Area

Sun Belt metros — Dallas–Fort Worth, Phoenix, Miami — grew well past their pre-pandemic baseline; older, slower-growing metros like Los Angeles and Philadelphia still hadn't caught up five years later.

Mapping an Uneven Recovery

To see whether this unevenness has a geographic logic, we built a proportional-symbol map: each metro area is a bubble sized by its 2019 passenger volume, colored on a red-to-blue scale by how fully it had recovered by 2025. The map makes visible what the bar chart above only implies — recovery clusters geographically. Blue, "recovered" bubbles concentrate in the South and Southwest, while red, "still behind" bubbles sit in the Northeast and along the Pacific coast.

This spatial pattern lines up with what demographers call the "Southward Expansion" — population and business activity migrating toward the Sun Belt. Gao (2022) clustered 118 U.S. hub airports by their pandemic recovery trajectories and found that geography significantly shaped recovery speed, confirming that recovery varied by region rather than proceeding uniformly. The lag in large coastal metros is also consistent with Javadinasr et al. (2022)'s finding that permanent increases in remote work were expected to reduce business air travel most in exactly the kind of corporate-travel-heavy hubs — Los Angeles, New York, Philadelphia — where our data shows the weakest recovery. Air travel, in other words, did not simply bounce back to where it had been; it followed people and capital toward where they were already heading.

Fewer Flights, Longer Routes

Our last question turns from who recovered to how the shape of the network itself changed. If airlines met demand with fewer, fuller flights, did they also change the kind of routes they flew? Average nonstop segment distance across our ten metro areas fell from about 1,069 miles in 2019 to 1,012 miles during the 2021 trough, before growing past its pre-pandemic level to roughly 1,102 miles by 2023 — even as total departures remained below their 2019 count.

Average Flight Distance vs. Total Departures

Departures dipped sharply in 2021 and were still recovering by 2023, while average segment length grew past its 2019 level, consistent with airlines consolidating capacity onto fewer, longer routes.

This finding echoes what Bao et al. (2021) describe at a global scale: the pandemic did not just shrink the world's air transportation network, it reorganized it, concentrating connectivity onto fewer, more heavily trafficked links. Sugishita, Mizutani, and Hanaoka (2024) find a similar pattern specific to U.S. domestic networks. Distinguishing route restoration from traffic concentration is, we think, one of the most important humanities insights of this project: a national passenger total recovering does not mean the geographic web connecting American communities recovered with it. Losing a route is not only a change in a transportation statistic — it is a weakening of the connection between families, workplaces, and regional economies that depended on it.

A Global Postscript: Did Happier Countries Fly Less?

Our T-100 data ends at the U.S. border, so for one final question we stepped outside it. If the pandemic's travel collapse was partly a matter of individual choice — fear, caution, compliance — might a country's collective mood have shaped how much its people kept flying? To find out, we joined two additional datasets: each country's 2020 life-evaluation score from the Gallup World Poll (surveyed mid-pandemic, published in the World Happiness Report) and the World Bank's count of air passengers carried, comparing 2019 against both 2020 (the collapse) and 2023 (the recovery).

National Happiness vs. the Air Travel Collapse, 2019–2020

Each dot is a country: its 2020 Gallup life-evaluation score against the change in air passengers carried from 2019 to 2020, colored by world region, with a linear trend line. Sources: World Happiness Report 2021 panel; World Bank (IS.AIR.PSGR).

The relationship is weakly negative (r ≈ −0.18) — and it runs opposite to the intuition that unhappy populations grounded themselves. The world's happiest countries during COVID, including Finland (7.9), Switzerland, and the Netherlands, suffered among the deepest collapses, losing roughly 70–80% of their passengers, while several lower-scoring countries such as China (−37%) declined far less. One plausible explanation is not happiness itself but what kind of flying each country depends on: the happiest countries are wealthy and internationally connected, and border-dependent international travel collapsed hardest and recovered slowest, while large domestic markets rebounded quickly — the same domestic/international split our passengers-per-departure chart shows inside the U.S. data.

National Happiness vs. the Recovery, 2019–2023

The same countries three years later: 2020 happiness against the change in passengers carried from 2019 to 2023. The association flattens further — recovery looks less like a function of national mood than of market structure.

Because happiness and travel were measured in the same period — both shaped by the pandemic itself — these charts can establish association only, not causation. The r-value and scatter imply correlation; the mechanism remains hypothetical without further surveys or analysis.

A caveat in the data

The World Bank attributes passengers to the country where the airline is registered, not where people actually fly — which is why hub and flag-carrier countries can look outsized, and why Denmark and Norway (served by the multinational SAS) are absent. We also excluded Zambia and the Dominican Republic from the recovery panel as artifacts of that method: neither had registered carriers before COVID, so their post-2020 "growth" of several hundred percent reflects newly registered airlines on near-zero 2019 baselines, not a real travel boom.

Conclusion

Read together, our visualizations, timeline, and map tell a story that a single "recovery" statistic cannot: American air travel did not simply pause and resume in 2020–2025. It collapsed by 61.8% almost overnight, came back on a faster, more concentrated schedule of fewer and fuller flights, retraced its old seasonal rhythm almost exactly, and redistributed itself geographically toward the Sun Belt while leaving several of its largest, oldest hubs still below their pre-pandemic baseline five years later. None of this is visible in the T-100 dataset's raw operational categories alone — as our Data Critique argues, the dataset was built to track industry throughput, not human experience. It took combining that operational record with secondary sources on remote work, regional tourism, and behavioral change to turn seven years of flight segments into an account of who was flying, why, and who got left on the ground.
